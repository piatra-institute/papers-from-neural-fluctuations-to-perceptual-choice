## Abstract

In models of choice, temperature is usually a parameter fitted to the choices it is meant to explain. For a decision variable that accumulates evidence to a bound, a quantity with the role of a temperature can be measured instead. The probability of a choice is a logistic function of the evidence $h$ divided by $D/(MB)$, where $D$ is the diffusion of the decision variable, $M$ its mobility (the change of drift per unit of evidence) and $B$ the bound, so the psychometric slope is $MB/D$. The ratio is unchanged by any affine change of the decoded coordinate and by the time unit, so a decoder in arbitrary units can be compared with behaviour. We give the exact factors by which leak, instability, trial-to-trial drift variability and start-point bias change the slope; leak can at most double it. For any stationary linear system, the fluctuation-response ratio along the Fisher discriminant of the stationary response is constant at every lag whether or not the system is at equilibrium, so its constancy along a trained decoder tests nothing. We estimate $D$ and $M$ from all trials through optional stopping and split-half cross moments, and test the estimators on a simulated population of 200 Poisson neurons carrying a bounded accumulator. One-bin increment variances predict a slope of 1.40 against a fitted 10.02, and variograms of undecided trials predict 12.29, while the stopped split-half estimators predict 9.89. Under leak, instability and drift variability the mean log ratio of fitted to predicted slope stays between -0.044 and 0.051; urgency and a slow signal along the coding axis that does not drive the choice raise it to 0.108 and 0.097. With 8,000 trials the run-to-run SD of the log ratio is 0.080; an equivalence test with a ±25% margin reaches a power of 0.8 at about 8,700 trials, and a ±10% margin needs about 48,000.

## 1. Introduction

Temperature appears in models of perception and choice as a parameter. A softmax rule with an inverse temperature is fitted to choices in reinforcement-learning tasks [@daw2006], and precision, an inverse variance that scales prediction errors, is inferred in models of attention [@feldman2010; @friston2010]. In each case the value comes from the behaviour that the model then explains. In physics temperature is a measured state variable. Einstein showed that the diffusion coefficient of a suspended particle and its mobility under a known force fix the temperature, $D = \mu k_B T$ [@einstein1905], and the fluctuation-dissipation theorem generalizes the relation to any observable conjugate to a weak perturbation [@callen1951; @kubo1966]. Out of equilibrium the ratio of fluctuation to response still defines an effective temperature, which can depend on time scale [@cugliandolo1997; @cugliandolo2011]; hair bundles of the inner ear violate the equilibrium relation, and their effective temperature diverges near the frequency of spontaneous oscillation [@martin2001]. The size of the violation measures dissipation [@harada2005; @baiesi2009; @seifert2012], and the same tests detect active processes in cytoskeletal networks and cells [@mizuno2007; @gnesotto2018] and broken detailed balance in human brain recordings [@lynn2021]. Responses of cortex to stimuli have been linked to its ongoing fluctuations as well [@arieli1996; @churchland2010; @sarracino2020].

A perceptual decision offers a setting where the analogue can be stated with all three quantities observable. In random-dot motion discrimination, neurons of the lateral intraparietal area (LIP) ramp at a rate set by motion strength and reach a common level before the saccade that reports the choice [@shadlen2001; @roitman2002]. Brief motion pulses have persistent effects on both choices and LIP activity [@huk2005], accumulation stops at a bound even when the environment controls viewing time [@kiani2008], and the accumulated quantity behaves like a log likelihood ratio [@gold2001; @gold2007; @yang2007; @kira2015]. The account is a bounded diffusion with roots in sequential analysis [@wald1945; @ratcliff1978; @ratcliff2008; @bogacz2006; @palmer2005; @shadlen2013]. The variance and correlation of LIP activity across trials match those of a diffusion [@churchland2011], single-trial decision variables can be decoded from populations [@kiani2014; @peixoto2021], and simultaneous recordings of up to 203 LIP neurons with Neuropixels probes show a drift-diffusion signal on single decisions [@steinemann2024; @stine2023]. Single-neuron spike trains have also been read as discrete steps [@latimer2015], LIP representations depend on task [@okazawa2021], and in a pulse-based task in rats the noise lay in sensory processing rather than in the accumulator [@brunton2013].

For a bounded accumulator the choice probability depends on diffusion, mobility and bound only through $D/(MB)$, a standard first-passage result [@palmer2005; @redner2001; @risken1989]. Each of the three can be estimated from a decoded neural decision variable on trials whose choices are not used, and the psychometric slope can be estimated from choices on other trials. The comparison is a test of the bounded-accumulation account with no parameter fitted to the outcome it predicts.

The relation is invariant under every affine change of the decoded coordinate and the time unit, and it transforms with the evidence unit as the fitted slope does. Leak, instability, drift variability and start-point bias change the slope by factors we derive in closed form; leak can at most double it. The equilibrium form of the test, a fluctuation-response ratio that is constant in lag, is uninformative along a trained decoder: for any stationary linear system it holds along the Fisher discriminant whether or not the system is at equilibrium. Optional stopping yields estimators of $D$ and $M$ that use all trials without bias from the absorbing bounds, and split-half cross moments remove observation noise. In a simulated spiking population the estimators predict the fitted slope without detectable bias, naive estimators fail by large factors, and the prediction stays close to behaviour under several departures from the integrator, which limits what agreement can show. We give the number of trials needed for an equivalence test and an analysis plan for a public dataset.

## 2. The first-passage relation

Let $X$ be a scalar decision variable that starts at $z = 0$ and follows

$$dX_t = \big(M h(t) - \lambda X_t\big)\,dt + \sqrt{2D}\,dW_t$$

until it reaches $+B$ or $-B$; the bound reached gives the choice. Here $h$ is the signed evidence (motion coherence in the examples), $M$ the mobility, $D$ the diffusion and $\lambda$ a leak, zero for the pure integrator. The decision time $\tau$ is the first exit time; the reaction time adds a non-decision time.

**Proposition 1.** For the pure integrator with constant evidence $h$, the probability of the choice $+$ is

$$P(+\mid h) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-h/\Theta}}, \qquad \Theta = \frac{D}{MB},$$

so the psychometric function is logistic with slope $\beta = MB/D$ per unit of evidence.

*Proof.* The exit probability $u(x)$ through $+B$ from a start $x$ solves $D u'' + Mh\,u' = 0$ with $u(-B) = 0$ and $u(B) = 1$, so $u(x) = (1 - e^{-Mh(x+B)/D})/(1 - e^{-2MhB/D})$. At $x = 0$ this is $1/(1 + e^{-MhB/D})$. $\square$

**Proposition 2.** Let the decoded variable be $A = aX + b$ with $a \neq 0$, let time be measured in a unit $\kappa$ times larger, and let evidence be measured in a unit $\gamma$ times larger. Then $D$, $M$ and $B$ measured in the new units give the same $D/(MB)$ when the coordinate or the time unit changes. When the evidence unit changes, $D/(MB)$ is divided by $\gamma$ and the fitted slope is multiplied by $\gamma$, so their product is unchanged. If $A = g(X)$ with $g$ nonlinear, the diffusion and mobility measured in $A$ are $g'(X)^2 D$ and $g'(X) M$, and their ratio varies with the state.

*Proof.* Per new unit of time the drift of $A$ is $a\kappa Mh$ and its diffusion $a^2\kappa D$, and the bound sits at distance $|a|B$ from the start, so $D/(MB)$ is multiplied by $a^2\kappa/(a\kappa|a|) = \pm 1$, the sign recording a relabelling of the choices. With evidence $h' = h/\gamma$ in the new unit the mobility per unit of $h'$ is $\gamma M$, so $D/(MB)$ is divided by $\gamma$, and $P(+) = 1/(1 + e^{-\gamma h'/\Theta})$ has slope $\gamma\beta$ per unit of $h'$. For a nonlinear $g$, Itô's formula gives a local diffusion $g'(X)^2 D$ and a local drift coefficient $g'(X) M$ plus a curvature term. $\square$

The proposition is what makes a neural test possible. A linear decoder of a population is known only up to scale and offset, and its output is in arbitrary units; $D$, $M$ and $B$ measured in those units still give $D/(MB)$ in units of evidence. A nonlinear readout destroys the invariance, so the decoder has to be linear in the decision variable over its range.

The relation is the Einstein relation in the coordinates of the decision. With evidence in the role of a force and $M$ in the role of mobility, $D/M$ has the role of $k_B T$, and the choice probability has the two-state Boltzmann form with the energy difference $h$ and the temperature $\Theta$, measured in units of evidence once divided by the bound. The analogy is exact for the pure integrator and fails for the departures below.

## 3. Departures from the integrator

The relation $\beta = MB/D$ holds for a particular stochastic process. We give the slope at zero evidence, $\beta_0 = 4\,dP/dh$ at $h = 0$, which equals the logistic slope when the function is logistic, for the standard departures.

**Proposition 3.** Relative to $MB/D$, the slope at zero evidence is multiplied by:

(a) for a leak $\lambda$, with the leak number $\Lambda = \lambda B^2/(2D)$,
$$r(\Lambda) = \frac{e^{\Lambda} - 1}{\Lambda \int_0^1 e^{\Lambda u^2}\,du},$$
which increases from 0 as $\Lambda \to -\infty$ (instability) through 1 at $\Lambda = 0$ to 2 as $\Lambda \to \infty$;

(b) for a drift that varies across trials with SD $\eta$, with $s = \eta B/D$, the factor $4\,E[\sigma'(sZ)]$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $Z$ a standard normal variable;

(c) for a start point $z$, the factor $1 - z^2/B^2$;

(d) for a start point uniform on $(-s_z, s_z)$, the factor $1 - s_z^2/(3B^2)$.

*Proof.* (a) With the scale density $\exp(-(vy - \lambda y^2/2)/D)$, the exit probability from 0 is the integral of the density over $(-B, 0)$ divided by its integral over $(-B, B)$. Differentiating at $v = 0$ gives $dP/dv = J/(DG)$ with $J = \int_0^B y\,e^{\lambda y^2/(2D)}\,dy = (B^2/(2\Lambda))(e^{\Lambda} - 1)$ and $G = 2B\int_0^1 e^{\Lambda u^2}\,du$; the integrator has $J/G = B/4$. For $\Lambda \to \infty$ the integral tends to $e^{\Lambda}/(2\Lambda)$, giving 2; for $\Lambda \to -\infty$ it tends to $\sqrt{\pi/(4|\Lambda|)}$, giving $2/\sqrt{\pi|\Lambda|} \to 0$. (b) $P = E[\sigma((Mh + \eta Z)B/D)]$; differentiate at $h = 0$ and use $\sigma'(0) = 1/4$. (c) Expanding the exit probability of Proposition 1 from $x = z$ to first order in $Mh$ gives $dP/dh = M(B^2 - z^2)/(4BD)$. (d) Average (c) over the start point. $\square$

The leak limit has a physical reading. With strong leak the decision variable equilibrates in the well around zero and leaves by escape over the barrier at either bound [@kramers1940]; the odds of the two exits then follow the Boltzmann ratio between the two bounds, whose energies differ by $2MBh$ in units of $D$, and the slope is $2MB/D$. The pure integrator has half that slope because it starts at the midpoint and exits before it equilibrates.

The closed forms agree with quadrature of the scale density to within $5 \times 10^{-7}$ for $\Lambda$ from -2 to 5 and with Monte Carlo simulation of 40,000 trials per condition. Table 1 gives values. A leak number of 1, which means the leak acts on the same time scale as diffusion across the bound, multiplies the slope by 1.175, the same instability multiplies it by 0.846, and drift variability with $s = 1$ by 0.826.

Bounds that collapse with time have no closed form. With $B(t) = B_0(1 - t/(t + t_{50}))$ and the reference parameters ($D = 0.8$ per s and $M = 8$ per unit coherence per s with $B_0 = 1$, a slope of 10 per unit coherence), the logistic slope fitted to 66,000 simulated choices over eleven coherences is 8.54, 7.82 and 6.94 for $t_{50}$ of 2, 1 and 0.5 s, against 9.90 for the fixed bound. Replacing $B_0$ by the mean bound $\bar B$ at the time of decision on trials below 10% coherence gives $M\bar B/D$ of 8.32, 7.52 and 6.61, slightly below the fitted slopes. A bound measured as the average termination level of the decoded variable therefore accounts for most of the effect of a collapse.

## 4. What the equilibrium form of the test measures

An equilibrium version of the test would record a decoded variable $A$ without evidence, compute its autocovariance $C(\tau)$, perturb with weak evidence to obtain the response function $R(\tau)$, and ask whether $-C'(\tau)/R(\tau)$ is constant in lag, as the fluctuation-dissipation theorem requires at equilibrium for the observable conjugate to the perturbation. For a one-dimensional stationary process $dX = (Mh - \lambda X)\,dt + \sqrt{2D}\,dW$ the ratio is $D/M$ at every lag, since $C(\tau) = (D/\lambda)e^{-\lambda\tau}$ and $R(\tau) = Me^{-\lambda\tau}$. For populations the result depends on the readout.

**Proposition 4.** Let $x$ follow $dx = (-Kx + fh)\,dt + \text{noise}$ with stationary covariance $S$, so that $C_w(\tau) = w^\top e^{-K\tau}Sw$ and $R_w(\tau) = w^\top e^{-K\tau}f$ for a readout $w$ and $\tau \ge 0$. Along $w^* = S^{-1}K^{-1}f$, the Fisher discriminant of the stationary responses to a constant $h$, $-C'_{w^*}(\tau) = R_{w^*}(\tau)$ for every $\tau$, with or without detailed balance. Under detailed balance $KS = D_x$, where $D_x$ is the diffusion matrix, and $w^*$ is proportional to $D_x^{-1}f$, the readout conjugate to the perturbation.

*Proof.* $Sw^* = K^{-1}f$, so $-C'_{w^*}(\tau) = w^{*\top}Ke^{-K\tau}K^{-1}f = w^{*\top}e^{-K\tau}f$ because $K$ commutes with $e^{-K\tau}$. Under detailed balance $S^{-1}K^{-1} = (KS)^{-1} = D_x^{-1}$. $\square$

The proposition removes the test from consideration. A decoder trained to discriminate responses to opposite evidence approximates $w^*$, and along $w^*$ the ratio is constant whatever the dynamics, so constancy says nothing about equilibrium or about a state variable. Along other readouts the ratio varies with lag even at equilibrium. In a two-dimensional example with a common stationary covariance (Figure 2), the conjugate readout and the Fisher discriminant coincide at detailed balance and the ratio is constant to rounding error along both, while along the first coordinate it varies by 29% and along the mean-response direction by 95% over the lags shown. With a rotational component that breaks detailed balance, the Fisher discriminant departs from the conjugate readout by 70°, the ratio along it remains constant, and the response along the conjugate readout changes sign. The first-passage relation avoids the difficulty, because the bound converts $D/M$ into evidence units and the prediction concerns choices, an outcome the decoder was not trained on.

## 5. Estimating diffusion, mobility and bound from a population

The decoded variable of a recorded population differs from $X$ in four ways: it is $A = cX + o + e$ with unknown scale $c$ and offset $o$; the observation noise $e$ from spiking is large in short bins; the trajectory stops at the bound, so trials still undecided at a late time are a selected subset; and the time of the bound crossing is known only through the reaction time.

**Proposition 5.** For the pure integrator started at zero, with $\tau$ the exit time and any fixed $t$,

$$E[X_{t\wedge\tau}] = E\Big[\int_0^{t\wedge\tau} Mh(s)\,ds\Big], \qquad E[X_{t\wedge\tau}^2] = 2D\,E[t\wedge\tau] \ \text{when } h = 0.$$

If two disjoint parts of the population give $A_k = c_k X + o_k + e_k$ with observation noise independent between the parts and over time, then $E[(A_1(t\wedge\tau) - A_1(0))(A_2(t\wedge\tau) - A_2(0))] = c_1 c_2 E[X_{t\wedge\tau}^2]$.

*Proof.* $X_t - \int_0^t Mh$ and, at $h = 0$, $X_t^2 - 2Dt$ are martingales; $t\wedge\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, so optional stopping applies. The cross moment follows from the independence of $e_1$, $e_2$ and $X$. $\square$

The proposition turns the absorbing bound from a source of bias into part of the estimator. A state-space model with point-process observations would also separate the two noises [@kalman1960; @smith2003], at the cost of a model of the dynamics whose errors enter $D$ and $M$. Each trial contributes its decoded value frozen at the decision, and the time measure is the decision time when the decision came first. In data the frozen value is taken from the post-decision period, before the saccade, and the decision time is the reaction time minus a non-decision time. Three estimators follow. $D$ comes from zero-evidence trials, as half the slope of the stopped split-half cross moment against the mean stopped time. $M$ comes either from sustained low evidence, as the slope of the mean of $h\,(A(t\wedge\tau) - A(0))$ against the mean of $h^2 (t\wedge\tau)$, or from pulses, as the slope of the onset-aligned stopped displacement against the stopped exposure to the pulse. $B$ is half the difference between the mean decoded levels before the saccade for the two choices. The scale factors cancel in $\hat D_{12}/(\hat M_1 \hat B_2)$, which we average with the estimate in which the parts are swapped. With binned data, a bin average of a Brownian path has second moment $2D(t_c - \Delta/6)$ for a bin of width $\Delta$ centred at $t_c$, and we use that time measure for bins before the decision.

We tested the estimators on a simulated population. The decision variable followed the integrator with $D = 0.8$ per s, $M = 8$ per unit coherence per s and $B = 1$, a slope of 10 per unit coherence and a mean decision time of 0.625 s at zero evidence, simulated in 2 ms steps with a Brownian-bridge test for crossings between steps. A population of 200 neurons had baseline rates between 15 and 35 Hz and gains on the decision variable proportional to their baselines, bounded so that rates stayed positive, and emitted Poisson counts in 20 ms bins. After the crossing the represented variable stayed at the bound until the saccade, 0.3 s later. Linear discriminants with strong shrinkage were trained on 400 trials at ±51.2% coherence, for the full population and for two random halves, and frozen. Separate trial sets served each estimate: 1,000 zero-coherence trials for $D$, 2,000 zero-coherence trials with a 100 ms pulse of ±20% coherence at a random onset between 0.1 and 0.5 s for the pulse estimate of $M$, 2,000 trials at ±6.4, 12.8 and 25.6% for the sustained estimate of $M$, and 3,000 trials over eleven coherences from -51.2 to 51.2% whose choices gave the fitted slope. No choice entered any neural estimate. Training trials always used Poisson counts; for the test trials most runs replaced the counts of each decoder by a Gaussian variable with the same mean and variance, and one comparison below checks the approximation.

Over 48 simulated datasets the slope fitted to choices was 10.02 (SD 0.44) and the stopped split-half estimators predicted 9.89 (SD 0.66). The mean log ratio of fitted to predicted slope was 0.014 (SE 0.011), with an SD of 0.079 across datasets. The estimates themselves were $D/B^2$ = 0.800 against 0.8 and $M/B$ = 7.90 against 8; the pulse estimate of $M/B$ was 8.28, with twice the spread of the sustained estimate. The naive estimator, the variance of one-bin increments of the full decoder on undecided trials, predicted a slope of 1.40, because spiking noise in a 20 ms bin dominates the diffusion of the decision variable. A variogram on undecided trials, which removes white observation noise through its intercept, predicted 12.29, too steep because trajectories still undecided at a given time have been selected away from the bounds and diffuse less (Figure 3).

(b) Slopes predicted by four estimators over 48 datasets (mean ± SD, logarithmic axis) against the slope fitted to choices (band).](../simulation/output/figures/estimators.png)

Three checks bound the remaining sources of error. Misstating the non-decision time by 50 ms shifts the mean log ratio from -0.018 to 0.029 (for 0.25 s) or -0.056 (for 0.35 s), so the non-decision time must be known to a few tens of milliseconds, for example from the neural signature of termination. With 50 neurons instead of 200 the SD of the log ratio rises from 0.079 to 0.133, and 400 neurons reduce it only to 0.074, because the latent diffusion dominates once observation noise is averaged out. Exact Poisson counts and the Gaussian approximation used for most runs give mean log ratios of -0.002 and -0.013 over 16 datasets each.

## 6. Prediction under six generating models

To ask what agreement shows, the same pipeline was applied to data generated by five departures from the integrator: leak ($\Lambda = 1$), instability ($\Lambda = -1$), drift variability across trials ($s = 1$), an urgency signal that multiplies the represented variable by $1 + t/(1\,\text{s})$ against a fixed neural bound, and a slow signal along the coding axis (SD 0.3 of the bound, time constant 1 s) that enters the neural readout but not the decision (Table 2, Figure 4).

The instantaneous parameters predict a slope of 10 in every case, against fitted slopes of 11.48, 8.66 and 8.51 for the leak, instability and drift-variability models. The stopped estimators do better because they measure an effective diffusion and mobility over the decision window: with leak the stopped second moment grows more slowly than $2Dt$, with instability faster, and the prediction moves in the direction of behaviour. Under these three models the mean log ratio stays between -0.044 and 0.051, and the prediction falls within ±25% of the fitted slope in at least 98% of datasets. Agreement between the neural prediction and behaviour is therefore evidence for bounded accumulation with the measured effective ratio; it does not single out the pure integrator.

## 7. Sample size

The comparison is an equivalence test: two one-sided tests at level 0.05 on the log ratio of fitted to predicted slope, with a margin chosen in advance [@schuirmann1987; @lakens2017]. Over 96 simulated datasets at each of 4,000, 8,000, 16,000 and 32,000 trials, with every trial set scaled in proportion, the SD of the log ratio was 0.106, 0.067, 0.069 and 0.041 (Figure 5). One curve $\kappa/\sqrt{n}$, with $n$ the number of trials and $\kappa$ = 7.12, describes the four values, which scatter around it by up to 23%, and we use the curve for the design statements. It gives an SD of 0.080 at 8,000 trials. Two one-sided tests with a margin of ±25% then reach a power of 0.8 at about 8,700 trials, and a margin of ±10% needs about 48,000 trials. Most of the uncertainty is on the neural side: at 8,000 trials the SD of the log fitted slope is 0.037 and that of the log predicted slope 0.066.

## 8. An analysis plan for public data

The data of Steinemann and colleagues fit the test closely [@steinemann2024; @steinemann2024data]. Two monkeys made reaction-time decisions about random-dot motion while up to 203 LIP neurons were recorded simultaneously, in eight sessions with 9,684 trials for one monkey and 8,142 for the other; on half of the trials a 100 ms pulse of weak motion appeared at a random time and biased choices and reaction times by the equivalent of 1.4% coherence. The authors fitted a race between two negatively correlated accumulators (correlation -0.71) with a linear urgency signal. The trial counts per monkey are close to the reference design of section 5, but the allocation of trials across coherences and pulses differs, so power has to be recomputed by simulation for the actual design. We propose the following plan, fixed on one monkey and run once on the other, with the code published before the confirmation run.

1. Decoder. A linear readout trained on strong-motion trials of the development monkey, frozen, with two disjoint halves of the population; the readout is checked for linearity by comparing termination levels across coherences.
2. Diffusion. The stopped split-half cross moment on 0% coherence trials without pulses, against the stopped time, with the non-decision time taken from the neural termination signature; the slope is reported for an early and a late window.
3. Mobility. The sustained estimate from low-coherence trials and the pulse estimate from pulse trials, reported separately; their ratio is a diagnostic.
4. Bound. Half the difference between choice-conditioned levels before the saccade, overall and by quintile of decision time, since the fitted urgency implies a level that changes with time.
5. Prediction. The slope $MB/D$ with a bootstrap interval over trials [@efron1993], compared with the slope fitted to held-out choices with a lapse rate [@wichmann2001] by two one-sided tests with a margin of ±25%, together with the late/early and pulse/sustained diagnostics.

The outcomes have distinct readings. Agreement within the margin with flat diagnostics supports bounded accumulation with the measured effective ratio. A prediction that is too low with a rising late/early ratio points to urgency, which the race fit already contains, and calls for a time-resolved prediction with the bound measured by decision time. A prediction that is too low with flat diagnostics points to a component of the readout that does not drive the choice, or to choice noise outside LIP. A prediction that is too high points to drift variability across trials or to readout noise that the split halves share. A manipulation of stimulus noise at fixed mean motion [@zylberberg2016] raises $D$ without changing $M$ and, if the bound stays fixed, should lower the slope in proportion, which tests the dependence on diffusion directly. Because the fitted model is a race, the relation of Proposition 1 holds for the difference of the accumulators only when their correlation is -1, so the plan has to reduce to a single variable by decoding that difference or replace Proposition 1 by the exit probability of the fitted race, computed numerically.

## 9. Limits

The propositions concern a one-dimensional decision variable; races of partially correlated accumulators and multidimensional representations need the exit probability of the fitted model, and the invariance of Proposition 2 holds only for linear readouts. The simulations use one reference parameter set, one population model with Poisson spiking and gains linear in the decision variable, and a decision variable that stays at the bound until the saccade; real LIP activity bursts before saccades and may not hold a constant level. The departures were applied one at a time, and combinations can cancel in the comparison. The estimators assume that observation noise is independent between the two halves of the population, which shared fluctuations of arousal or of other latent variables would violate; the aligned-nuisance model is one such case. The evidence unit, coherence, is a property of the stimulus, so the measured ratio is not comparable across tasks or modalities until evidence is expressed in a common unit such as a log likelihood ratio [@kira2015]. Precision in active-inference models [@feldman2010] could be compared with $1/\Theta$ in the same way, but whether those models make that identification is a question about the models [@aguilera2022; @isomura2023]. No data were analyzed. The analysis plan applies to a public dataset [@steinemann2024data], and the same pipeline could run on recordings from other species and tasks, such as the mouse visual task of the International Brain Laboratory [@ibl2021], once a stimulus variable with a defined evidence scale is available.
